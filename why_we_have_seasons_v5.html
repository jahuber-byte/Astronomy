Why We Have Seasons

Earth travels around the Sun while its 23.5° axial tilt keeps nearly the same orientation relative to the distant stars. That changes both sunlight angle and daylight duration during the year.
Astronomy • Seasons concept model • Version 5
Central idea: Earth's axis does not “flop” back and forth during the year. It stays nearly parallel to itself in space. As Earth moves around the Sun, each hemisphere alternately tilts toward and away from the Sun.
The orbit is shown nearly edge-on. June is at the left, December at the right; September passes in front of the Sun and March passes behind it. The eye symbol follows Earth along its orbit and shows the moving viewpoint used in Pane 2.

What to watch

1. Follow the green axisIn Pane 1 it keeps the same direction all year, even as Earth moves from one side of the Sun to the other. The eye shows that Pane 2's viewing direction is the part that changes.
2. Compare June and DecemberAt June the Northern Hemisphere tilts toward the Sun; at December it tilts away. Notice which half of Earth is illuminated.
3. Use Pane 2 as a comparison viewThe moving camera follows Earth along the orbit, so its perspective rotates through the year. Use Pane 2 to compare Sun angle and daylight while remembering that the physical axis remains fixed in space.

Misconception check

Earth is closest to the Sun in early January. Why is January still winter in Minnesota but summer in Australia?

The seasons are controlled mainly by axial tilt. In January the Northern Hemisphere is tilted away from the Sun, giving lower Sun angles and shorter days, while the Southern Hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun. The small change in Earth–Sun distance is not the cause of the seasons.
Model limits: This is a two-dimensional representation of a three-dimensional situation. Pane 1 deliberately shows the ecliptic nearly edge-on so the 23.5° axial tilt is easy to compare with the orbital plane. Pane 2 is a moving, rotated comparison view from a point following Earth in its orbit, not the same fixed camera angle as Pane 1. Earth's size, the Sun's size, and orbital distances are not to scale.
